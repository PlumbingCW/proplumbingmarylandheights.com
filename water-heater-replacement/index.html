Emergency steps

A flooded tank: the first hour

A failed tank is messy but manageable if the first few steps are done correctly.

Do these before the plumber arrives

  • Close the cold inlet valve, or the main shutoff if that valve will not turn.
  • Turn off power at the breaker. An electric element burns out within minutes if the tank runs dry.
  • Set a gas control to off and ventilate if the closet is closed in.
  • Run a hose from the drain valve to a floor drain only if the water is cool. Hot water scalds.
  • Photograph the tank, the label and the water damage for your insurance claim.

Sudden tank failure is sometimes covered by homeowner policies as water damage, but wear and corrosion usually are not. Ask your insurer which applies to you.

Replacing a water heater: what a visit involves

The exact sequence varies with the house, but the major steps are the same.

  1. Confirm the replacement

    The plumber checks the tank, the venting and the water and gas or electric supply, then confirms the size and fuel type with you.

  2. Drain and disconnect

    Water, power and gas are shut off, the tank is drained and the old unit is disconnected from the pipes and vent.

  3. Set the new heater

    The new tank goes on a level base, often with a drain pan and a discharge line from the relief valve.

  4. Connect and test

    Supply lines, valves and gas or electric connections go on, then the tank is filled and tested for leaks and venting.

  5. Check the safety devices

    The relief valve, expansion allowance and flue draft are checked before the unit is left running.

You decide on the plan before work begins, and we explain what the permit step involves if your job needs one.

Sizing the new tank for your household

A tank that is too small runs out in the middle of a shower, and one that is too large wastes energy keeping extra water hot. The right size depends on how many people live in the house and when they use hot water.

One to two people30 to 40 gallonsSmaller tank is fine if showers are staggered

Tell the dispatcher how many bathrooms you have and whether a soaking tub is in the house. We will suggest a size, and our tankless water heater page explains when an on-demand unit makes more sense.

Tank, tankless or heat pump

A conventional tank is the simplest replacement because it uses the same connections as the old unit. A tankless heater provides continuous hot water but needs correct venting, enough gas or electric capacity and regular descaling. A heat pump water heater uses less electricity but needs clearance, a condensate drain and a space that stays above freezing.

If you want to move from gas to a heat pump, tell the dispatcher so we can look at the electric panel and the room before quoting. We do not promise rebates or savings figures, because incentives change and depend on your utility and your house.

Permits and Maryland Heights rules

Ask the building division

The City of Maryland Heights residential plumbing permits page says a permit can be pulled without plans for replacing water heaters up to 120 gallons. That means a standard home replacement is a permit job that does not need drawn plans, though a permit is still a step in the process.

Call the Community Development building division at (314) 291-6550 and ask what applies to your job. We explain the permit step before work starts, so there are no surprises when the inspector is scheduled.

Sudden failure or planned replacement

A tank near the end of its life often shows signs. Rust in the hot water, water pooling at the base and a rumbling burner or element are all reasons to schedule water heater installation before the leak arrives.

A planned replacement gives you time to choose the model, the size and the day. An emergency replacement still goes smoothly when you can describe the existing tank, but you have fewer options in a hurry.

Codes and standards

Permits, codes and the tank itself

The City of Maryland Heights says a permit can be pulled without plans for water heaters up to 120 gallons. Under the codes the new tank needs a working temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe drain, and gas units need a vent that meets the manufacturer's installation standard.

If you want the code text itself, the International Plumbing Code (IPC) is the usual model, though the city decides which edition it enforces. The building division can say which one governs a tank swap at your address.
